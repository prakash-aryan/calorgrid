HeatShift · compute that follows the cold01
Open with the one-line idea: a server is a 1 kW heater that happens to compute. The product challenge is scheduling, because compute demand is year-round while useful heat demand is seasonal.
1 · The case for action

Heat is already being paid for. Compute is already being sold.

DigitalHeat can create a second revenue stream from the same electricity by putting servers where their waste heat replaces a heat pump or boiler.

Building owner

“I need reliable heat, but a server rack sounds like a new operational risk.”

Concern: comfort, noise, uptime, safety, and who pays the power bill.

Compute buyer

“I need capacity every day, but I cannot accept unpredictable pauses.”

Concern: missed deadlines, repeated work, and opaque performance.

Our wedge: make the trade-off visible and controllable. The scheduler sells computing only when the building can use the heat, then moves or pauses work before comfort or SLA risk appears.
Problem framing · customer tension02
Do not pitch this as “free heat”. The customer buys a controlled energy service. Heat value is real only when the building would otherwise run its heating system.
2 · Product thesis

The platform coordinates three clocks

A building has a heat clock. A customer job has a deadline clock. A site has a power and cost clock. HeatShift allocates work where all three line up.

01 · SENSE

Read the building

Hourly heat demand, tank state, weather and absences define the safe heat budget.

02 · VALUE

Price the hour

Electricity, heat value, server cost and job revenue produce a break-even signal.

03 · PLAN

Admit work

Use a 36-hour forecast, a 70% trust factor and least-laxity-first scheduling.

04 · ACT

Run or pause

Allocate, throttle, pause with SIGSTOP, or checkpoint and migrate with SIGTERM.

05 · PROVE

Report the balance

Show useful heat, dumped heat, coverage, missed deadlines, utilisation and margin.

3 · Where the first customers are

Sell heat where demand is predictable and operational access is easy

The first markets should have a clear heat sink, staff who can approve a pilot, and compute work that tolerates interruption.

HHospitals

Use recovered heat for domestic hot water, staff housing or a non-critical building loop. Keep clinical systems outside the pilot boundary.

Strong fit: 24/7 hot-water base load
Pilot proof: isolation, redundancy, clinical safety review

CChurches

Serve intermittent occupancy: preheat before services, pause between events, and schedule community or archive compute when the building is occupied.

Strong fit: flexible schedules
Pilot proof: low-temperature comfort and noise limits

TTrain stations

Anchor a station-adjacent room, office, or retail heat loop. Shift work to commuter peaks and use forecast demand for concourses and staff areas.

Strong fit: public visibility, year-round load
Pilot proof: security, access, and network resilience

PIndoor pools

Use long opening hours and a large hot-water demand to absorb server heat. The app’s flat demand shape already models pools and hotels.

Strong fit: long heat window
Pilot proof: humidity, corrosion, maintenance

AAlpine hotels

Route summer and shoulder-season workloads to sites that still need heat. The map already surfaces alpine sites as year-round heat sinks.

Strong fit: seasonal compute migration
Pilot proof: connectivity and service access

RRental housing

Start with a single reference house or small block. Offer the owner transparent heat value while DigitalHeat keeps the compute service contract.

Strong fit: repeatable deployment model
Pilot proof: tenant consent and landlord split
Business use cases · hypotheses to validate04
Hospitals and train stations are enterprise pilots, not claims that we are ready to connect to critical infrastructure. Church and housing pilots are a lower-risk way to prove comfort and acceptance.
4 · Customer value

One control plane, two contracts, three measurable outcomes

Heat customer

Lower useful heat cost

Heat is valued at electricity price divided by the heat-pump COP. The homeowner or facility gets a transparent credit for heat actually used.

Compute customer

Flexible capacity

SLA work runs inside a heat-aware budget. Backfill work fills remaining capacity at a lower spot price, with checkpoints for migration.

DigitalHeat

Better utilisation

Revenue comes from computing while heat revenue reduces net cost. Site placement and scheduling decide whether capex earns its return.

7 · The network effect

Site selection turns seasonal heat into a routing problem

A single house has a winter peak and a summer trough. A portfolio can move jobs to sites whose heat demand remains useful.

Winter routing

Lowland homes provide the largest heat sink. Schedule deadline work close to the demand and use the tank to absorb short peaks.

8 · How the app was built

From weather and jobs to a live, auditable control loop

DATA

Inputs

Open-Meteo weather, ElCom tariffs, HSR profiles, MLPerf throughput and Alibaba job shapes.

MODEL

Building demand

Degree-hour space heating plus hot-water shape. Holidays lower setpoint and hot-water draw.

SCHEDULER

Heat budget

Tank state, forecast admission, least-laxity-first, atomic jobs and proactive migration.

LIVE

Real process

Same allocation call controls a job: SIGSTOP/SIGCONT and checkpoint/resume.

UI

Decision view

Live demo, yearly balance, policies, map, economics, edge cases, and assumptions.

Why this is credible

Results are reproducible from a single assumptions file. Tests cover energy balance, tank limits, zero dump, and checkpoint/resume.

What the app does not hide

2025 is one weather year, the tank model is lossless, GPU power is an assumption, and market prices are ranges. Those are visible design boundaries.

9 · Pilot plan

Prove the commercial loop in four controlled steps

0–30 days
Measure

  • Pick one non-critical heat loop.
  • Install sensors and define comfort limits.
  • Collect a baseline of heat, power and occupancy.

31–60 days
Shadow

  • Run HeatShift beside existing heating.
  • Replay workloads without controlling the building.
  • Compare predicted and actual demand.

61–90 days
Control

  • Enable a capped server fleet.
  • Use zero-dump mode and a human override.
  • Report SLA, comfort, savings and incidents weekly.

90+ days
Scale

  • Add a second site with a different demand shape.
  • Test migration between sites.
  • Convert the pilot to a heat plus compute contract.

First pilot candidate

Indoor pool or hotel: large, predictable hot-water demand, operational staff on site, and a strong reason to keep compute useful outside the winter peak.

Execution · from demo to deployment10
The first deployment should be deliberately boring. Avoid a clinical heat loop or a critical station control system until the operating model and failover story are proven.
10 · Open questions

What we should keep open until the pilot gives us evidence

Commercial design

Should the homeowner receive a fixed heat credit, a share of compute revenue, or a guaranteed heat price? Who owns the hardware at contract end?

Reliability boundary

What heat loop can safely accept a server as a secondary source? What is the required backup capacity and manual override process?

Workload mix

Which customers will sign for interruptible work? What percentage of jobs are atomic, and what SLA premium makes checkpointing worthwhile?

Regulation and acceptance

What approvals cover fire safety, water quality, noise, data residency, tenant consent, and insurance?

Scaling model

When do we aggregate houses into a virtual heat sink? When is a pool, hotel, or district loop more efficient than another residential site?

Measurement

Which meter becomes the source of truth for useful heat, customer savings, compute revenue, and avoided emissions?
